Columbia classification (2004): Five mutually exclusive light-microscopy variants assigned by hierarchy.
Collapsing → Tip → Cellular → Perihilar → NOS
A single collapsing glomerulus is enough to call the collapsing variant. The classification applies to both primary and secondary FSGS but cannot determine etiology—EM foot process effacement and clinical context are needed.

💥 Collapsing Worst prognosis

~13% remission
~65% ESRD; 3-year renal survival 33%

Histology: ≥1 glomerulus with segmental or global collapse of capillaries and hypertrophy/hyperplasia of overlying podocytes.

Associations: HIV (HIVAN), APOL1 high-risk genotype, CMV, EBV, parvovirus, SARS-CoV-2; interferon, pamidronate; younger Black patients; severe nephrotic syndrome, renal insufficiency at presentation.

Practical implication: Urgently look for secondary/genetic cause and treat it. Immunosuppression response poor (~14% rituximab response). Some series report up to 60% remission—primary collapsing may still get steroid/CNI trial.

🌱 Tip Best prognosis

~76% remission
~6% ESRD; steroid-responsive

Histology: ≥1 segmental lesion in the tip domain (outer 25% of tuft next to proximal tubule origin), with no collapsing or perihilar lesions.

Associations: Usually primary, abrupt nephrotic syndrome, MCD-like; older, more often White patients; diffuse foot process effacement.

Practical implication: Behaves like minimal change disease. High steroid responsiveness and best remission rates. Rituximab response best here (100% relapse-free survival in one cohort).

🔵 Cellular Intermediate

~45% remission
~28% ESRD

Histology: ≥1 glomerulus with segmental endocapillary hypercellularity occluding lumina, ± foam cells and karyorrhexis; tip and collapsing excluded.

Associations: Primary or secondary; least common; usually nephrotic; may be an early lesion or an unsampled tip/collapsing lesion.

Practical implication: Usually nephrotic → standard primary FSGS treatment. Intermediate response (44–45% remission). Over-represented among rituximab non-responders.

🏛️ Perihilar Intermediate

Adaptive/secondary
Responds to RAAS blockade

Histology: Perihilar sclerosis/hyalinosis in >50% of segmentally sclerosed glomeruli; ≥1 with perihilar hyalinosis.

Associations: Obesity, hypertension, reflux, reduced nephron mass; subnephrotic proteinuria, often with glomerulomegaly.

Practical implication: Strongly suggests adaptive/secondary FSGS. Use supportive therapy only (RAAS blockade, SGLT2i, weight loss); avoid immunosuppression. Lowest remission rates.

❓ NOS Intermediate

Most common
~42–48% of cases

Histology: Segmental matrix increase obliterating capillary lumina; other variants excluded.

Associations: Primary or secondary; often subnephrotic; all variants converge to NOS with progression.

Practical implication: Decide by etiology and nephrotic status. If primary, standard steroid → CNI pathway. Relatively good rituximab response in one cohort.

⚠️ Key caveats:
  • Prognostic gradient: best in tip, intermediate in cellular/perihilar/NOS, worst in collapsing. A Japanese registry (n=304) found no significant 5-year outcome difference between variants. Achieving proteinuria remission predicted outcome regardless of variant (adjusted HR 0.19).
  • Mixed lesions and evolution: one biopsy can show several lesion types, and lesions evolve over time to NOS. Adequate sampling matters.
  • Stronger outcome predictors than variant: interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy, global sclerosis, baseline creatinine, and remission status.
  • Distinguishing etiology: diffuse foot process effacement on EM with nephrotic syndrome favors primary FSGS. Segmental effacement favors maladaptive FSGS. Genetic forms can show either.
  • Guidelines do not assign different drug regimens by variant: treatment is based on etiology (primary vs genetic vs secondary) and phenotype (nephrotic or not). Variant informs expected response, urgency of workup, and persistence.

When to order genetic testing?

✅ Strongly indicated

  • Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome / FSGS
  • Early onset (childhood / young adult)
  • Family history of FSGS or ESKD
  • APOL1 high-risk suspicion (African ancestry, collapsing FSGS, HIVAN)

Genetic variants detected in 35.5% of SRNS-FSGS or FSGS-UC in one adult cohort; 41.7% in SRNS-FSGS specifically. KDIGO: steroid resistance should prompt genetic testing.

🧬 Why test?

  • Avoid futile immunosuppression
  • Predict low recurrence after transplant (monogenic causes)
  • Inform living-donor evaluation (APOL1)
  • Identify trial eligibility (e.g., inaxaplin for APOL1)

KDIGO 2025 Controversies Conference: genotype alone not actionable, but supports risk stratification and donor evaluation.

🧪 APOL1 genotyping

~13% of African Americans carry two risk alleles (G1/G2). Two variants raise FSGS risk 10–17-fold.

Consider in: collapsing FSGS, HIVAN, family history, transplant donor evaluation.

A “second hit” (e.g., interferon, HIV) often required.

Practical trigger: Any patient with FSGS who is steroid-resistant or has a family history should be offered genetic testing. In adults, panel testing (COL4A3-5, NPHS2, INF2, etc.) yields ~35% diagnostic rate.
